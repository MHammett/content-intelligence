# Data Centers Don't Have an Environmental Record. They Have Eight of Them.

*Mike Hammett has operated ISP infrastructure in Northern Illinois for two decades and co-founded FD-IX. He holds no material financial interest in any data center operator.*

---

## TL;DR

- Data center environmental impact varies by grid, cooling technology, geography, and siting. Most public claims strip that context out entirely.
- The air quality problem in Northern Virginia is approximately 4,700 diesel backup generators permitted in Loudoun County alone, concentrated in an ozone nonattainment zone. The Virginia electrical grid runs below the national average for NOx emissions. Those two facts are routinely conflated. [1][4][4a]
- U.S. data centers consumed 17 billion gallons of water directly in 2023, equal to about 0.04% of national agricultural irrigation withdrawals. U.S. golf courses use roughly 31 times that amount annually for irrigation. The Great Lakes basin is not the same place as Phoenix or Las Vegas. [7][8][20]
- The worst recent operator conduct is documented and serious: xAI is alleged to have operated dozens of unpermitted gas turbines near Memphis and in Southaven, Mississippi; QTS drew 29 million unmetered gallons in Fayette County, Georgia. Neither is representative of compliant facilities.
- Virginia's own legislative study found most data centers generate no documented noise complaints. The Piedmont Environmental Council submitted an air quality analysis that modeled maximum permitted emissions as operational norms and aggregated sources that do not run simultaneously. Virginia DEQ published a formal rebuttal. Neither finding got the coverage the original PEC report received. [4][19][19a]

## The Core Problem

Most environmental claims about data centers are accurate somewhere. Almost none of them specify where.

A noise complaint from Chandler, Arizona, a nitrate contamination lawsuit from Morrow County, Oregon, and a diesel generator study from Loudoun County, Virginia, all describe real documented problems. When those stories travel into community meetings in Northern Illinois, the geographic conditions that made them true don't come along. The grid mix doesn't come along. The cooling technology doesn't come along. The pre-existing regulatory failures and nonattainment designations don't come along.

What comes along is the conclusion.

The industry is not blameless here. Operators have used nondisclosure agreements to prevent local governments from disclosing basic project terms to residents. They have marketed annual renewable energy certificate accounting as "100% renewable" power. They have published inflated job projections that were later revised down by 70%. They have been silent when other operators committed permit violations that should have drawn industry condemnation. The information vacuum the industry creates through opacity fills with whatever the opposition supplies first. This publication has addressed the transparency problem directly, including a call for operators to stop using NDAs as a condition of community engagement. [23]

But the opposition has its own problems, and those get less scrutiny. Claims about data center environmental impact have circulated without correction, been amplified by media coverage that did not check the underlying analysis, and in at least one documented case were submitted to state regulators in a report those regulators formally rebutted with primary data.

What follows addresses eight environmental categories. For each, the question is the same: what does documented evidence show, what determines whether the concern applies in a specific geography, and where does the claim go beyond what the evidence supports?

The tier labels used throughout:

**Systemic** — documented at compliant facilities across multiple geographies.
**Regional** — real, but only under specific geographic or operational conditions.
**Isolated** — documented at one or few facilities due to misconduct or atypical design.
**Exaggerated** — the concern is real but the figures represent permit ceilings or worst-case scenarios, not operational norms.
**Selective** — a legitimate concern applied only to data centers, not to comparable land uses.
**Misattributed** — the harm is real but the source, geography, or cause is wrong.
**Contested** — a plausible mechanism exists but causal documentation at compliant facilities does not.
**Fabricated** — no credible evidentiary basis exists.

A note on source evaluation. This article cites government filings, peer-reviewed literature, legislative studies, and primary utility documents throughout. Where advocacy organizations are cited on either side of the debate, they are identified as such in the citation.
Two sourcing patterns appear frequently enough in the public discussion of data center environmental claims that they are worth identifying before they appear elsewhere in the coverage a reader may encounter.
The first is the difference between a permitted emission ceiling and an operational norm. A permit that authorizes a facility to run generators 1,000 hours per year does not predict that generators will run 1,000 hours per year. Virginia's JLARC found the state's data center generator fleet operating at roughly 7% of permitted capacity during the monitoring period. [4] Reports that model permitted ceilings as if they represent operational reality, without disclosing that assumption, produce figures that are technically authorized but operationally misleading. The ceiling and the operational number are not the same measurement.
The second is abbreviation overlap that creates a false impression of evidentiary authority. "EHP" appears in environmental health discussions as shorthand for two distinct organizations: Environmental Health Perspectives, the peer-reviewed journal published by the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences and indexed in PubMed, and the Environmental Health Project, a Pennsylvania-based advocacy organization focused on unconventional gas development and industrial siting impacts. The two organizations have different evidentiary standards, different institutional structures, and different relationships to the outcomes they study. A citation to one is not a citation to the other. In coverage of data center health claims in Pennsylvania specifically, verifying which organization is the actual source is a necessary step before evaluating what the citation supports.

## Air Quality: The Grid

*Tier: Regional*

Grid-connected emissions from a data center depend entirely on the grid it connects to. This is the first thing most commentary omits, and it renders much of the air quality debate incoherent.

A note on terminology: a power company is the utility that sells you electricity. A grid region is the geographic footprint of shared transmission infrastructure. An RTO (Regional Transmission Organization) is the independent body that operates that infrastructure (PJM, MISO, ERCOT, and others) which typically does not align with state lines or utility territories. One state can span multiple RTOs. One RTO can span a dozen states. The eGRID subregions in the table below are EPA's emissions accounting zones, which roughly follow RTO boundaries. The table covers regions directly relevant to data center build-out activity and to the Northern Illinois context. For the full national picture, EPA's eGRID explorer at epa.gov/egrid covers every U.S. subregion.

EPA's eGRID 2023 data shows a factor-of-four spread in NOx output across U.S. grid regions. [1] The grid serving Northern Illinois, Ohio, and Indiana (RFCW) produces 0.40 lbs of NOx per MWh. The grid serving Missouri and Kentucky (SRMW) produces 0.80 lbs per MWh. The Virginia grid (RFCE) produces 0.20 lbs per MWh. The national average is 0.50. Illinois specifically generates 53% of its electricity from nuclear and 13% from wind, putting the state's actual NOx output well below the RFCW subregion average. [2]

The grid in Indiana and Kentucky is where the air quality argument against data centers has real technical merit. SRMW produces 0.80 lbs NOx and 1.64 lbs SO2 per MWh. In coal-heavy grids like that one, a modern behind-the-meter natural gas plant can be demonstrably cleaner than the marginal generator the facility would otherwise draw from the grid. That is the one geography where industry claims about cleaner on-site generation are technically defensible. [1]

In Illinois, with a nuclear-dominant grid, behind-the-meter gas is worse than the average grid on every emissions measure. This is not a close call.

*Tier: Misattributed — when grid emissions from coal-heavy regions are applied to nuclear-dominant Illinois*

Applying air quality concerns calibrated to Indiana's grid to a proposal in DeKalb County is not analysis. It is the wrong number in the wrong place.

## Air Quality: Diesel Generators

*Tier: Regional — Northern Virginia cumulative generator concern*

The air quality problem in Northern Virginia is not the grid. The RFCE grid runs below the national NOx average. The problem is approximately 4,700 diesel backup generators permitted in Loudoun County alone, the core of a geography already designated as nonattainment under the 2015 ozone NAAQS. [3][4][4a]

Virginia's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission published its study of data centers in December 2024 (RD206). [4] It found that generators contribute less than 4% of regional NOx from point sources during actual operations, a fraction of their permitted ceiling, which they run at roughly 7% of permitted capacity during the monitoring period. The study also found that no comprehensive cumulative impact analysis has been conducted for the generator fleet, that roughly one-third of Virginia data centers sit within 200 feet of residential zones, and that standard permitting evaluates each facility individually rather than as part of a regional cluster drawing on a shared airshed.

The gap between "less than 4% of regional NOx" and "no one has modeled what happens if the cluster activates simultaneously during a grid outage" is where the legitimate concern lives. Generator testing is staggered precisely to avoid simultaneous load and emissions spikes; each unit gets its compliance hours on a schedule. A real grid outage is the scenario where all of them run at once. The regulatory framework was not designed for this concentration at this scale, and the cumulative analysis of simultaneous activation during an outage has not been done.

*Tier: Misattributed — when the Virginia generator concern is applied to Illinois*

That concern does not transfer to the counties where data center buildout is actually concentrated. DeKalb County and the non-Oswego portions of Kendall County are in attainment for both ozone and PM2.5 under current NAAQS; they are outside the Chicago nonattainment area boundary entirely. [5][5a] Current EPA stationary engine emission standards apply under RICE NESHAP (40 CFR Part 63 Subpart ZZZZ) to new permitted engines. Annual testing for emergency engines is capped at 100 hours. [5] There is no pre-existing nonattainment problem in the DeKalb County corridor for a generator cluster to compound.

*Tier: Selective — generator emissions scrutinized; comparable combustion sources are not*

The scale of the concern also deserves context. The U.S. Department of Transportation estimates that Class 8 truck idling produces roughly 11 million tons of CO2 annually across the national fleet. [22] Data center backup generators under standard EPA testing regimes run approximately 50 to 100 hours per year for compliance testing. They also run during actual grid outages, which are infrequent by design. At those combined hours, the national generator fleet across thousands of facilities produces a fraction of what major interstate truck corridors produce from idling alone. This does not make the Northern Virginia cumulative NOx question less legitimate, and it is legitimate. But the generators in a well-sited, code-compliant facility in Illinois are not the same object as the Virginia cluster, and neither one belongs in the same category as combustion sources that draw no comparable regulatory attention. [22]

*Tier: Isolated — unpermitted operation; deliberate pattern, not representative of compliant facilities*

The xAI situation belongs in a different category entirely. Beginning in June 2024, xAI installed as many as 35 gas turbines at its Colossus 1 facility in Memphis without obtaining required Clean Air Act permits, according to SELC and regulatory filings. After the Southern Environmental Law Center gave notice of intent to sue on behalf of the NAACP, xAI removed most of the turbines and obtained a permit for 15 remaining units. [6a] The company did not change its approach. It built Colossus 2 in Southaven, Mississippi, just across the state line. The NAACP and Earthjustice filed a Clean Air Act suit in April 2026, alleging operation of 27 unpermitted turbines that could emit more than 1,700 tons of NOx annually. [6b] No final court order or agency finding has been issued as of publication.

Both facilities sit in the Memphis metro area, which has documented air quality problems and a disproportionate concentration of industrial pollution in the communities affected. The alleged conduct, if proven, is not a paperwork oversight. It is a deliberate pattern of operating unpermitted combustion equipment in an area with pre-existing air quality problems, removing it under legal threat, and replicating the same approach across the state line.

Note on cumulative permitting: the tier system above evaluates individual facilities. A structural feature of the Clean Air Act permitting framework cuts across the air quality categories and is worth naming directly. The Clean Air Act evaluates sources individually against major source thresholds of 100 to 250 tons per year depending on source category and attainment status. A data center campus with 40 diesel generators, each permitted as a minor source below that threshold, is never evaluated as a single aggregate emitter. In a county where multiple campuses are permitted on overlapping timelines, no required process assesses what the combined generator fleet means for the local airshed. Virginia's JLARC acknowledged this explicitly: no comprehensive cumulative impact analysis has been conducted for the Loudoun County generator fleet. [4] The gap is procedural. In attainment counties like DeKalb, ambient monitoring already captures the aggregate airshed condition, and attainment status is a partial check that individual permit review does not provide. But monitoring station density is insufficient to detect localized concentration hotspots between stations, and the simultaneous-activation scenario during a real grid outage has not been modeled in any data center geography. The absence of that analysis is not evidence that harm is occurring. It is evidence that the regulatory framework was not designed for this concentration at this scale.

## Water Supply

*Tier: Selective — when applied without context to national aggregate figures*

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's congressionally mandated 2024 report found that U.S. data centers consumed 17 billion gallons of water directly in 2023 through on-site cooling systems. [7] U.S. agricultural irrigation withdrawals total roughly 118 billion gallons per day, or approximately 43 trillion gallons annually. [8] Data centers' 17 billion gallons of direct annual consumption represents about 0.04% of that annual agricultural figure.

For comparison: U.S. golf courses used approximately 1.63 million acre-feet of water for irrigation in 2024, equal to roughly 531 billion gallons, according to the GCSAA's peer-reviewed national survey. [20] That is approximately 31 times what data centers consume directly. Both categories use water. One of them absorbs national coverage as a water crisis. The difference is not scale. It is what the building type looks like.

*Tier: Regional — real stress in specific geographies*

The national aggregate conceals real local stress in specific places. Google's Council Bluffs, Iowa, campus accounted for roughly 21% of the city's total water supply in 2023, according to a peer-reviewed paper in AGU Advances. [9] In Newton County, Georgia, a single Meta facility consumes roughly 500,000 gallons per day, approximately 10% of the entire county's daily consumption, while the county continues to receive additional data center permit applications that would more than double total countywide use. [10] Phoenix, Las Vegas, Reno, and West Texas face structural scarcity. These are genuine problems in specific places.

*Tier: Misattributed — when arid-geography water stress is applied to Illinois*

Northern Illinois draws from Lake Michigan and productive aquifers within the Great Lakes basin. Making a claim about data center water consumption in DeKalb County based on conditions in Maricopa County is not an honest argument.

*Tier: Exaggerated — when permit-ceiling figures are cited as operational norms*

Cooling technology is the other variable that disappears from most coverage. Evaporative cooling towers use substantially more water than closed-loop air cooling, and the distinction matters more than facility size.

Project Cardinal in Yorkville illustrates this directly. The original water-cooled design projected 350,000 gallons per day across a 1,037-acre campus, roughly 338 gallons per acre per day. [11a] Even at that figure, the original design already used less water per acre than actual Yorkville residential subdivisions. Grande Reserve has approximately 2,200 planned homes on roughly 550 acres of residential lots (about 4 homes per acre), producing an estimated 960 gallons per acre per day at standard Midwest residential water use rates. Autumn Creek has 498 homes on roughly 134 acres of residential lots, about 3.7 homes per acre, producing an estimated 890 gallons per acre per day. [11d] These figures are derived from confirmed unit counts and average lot sizes from public records and listing data; they are the author's own calculations, not metered utility data. The city's own aggregate residential baseline reported to Shaw Local is approximately 600 gallons per acre per day; that figure is pulled down by commercial zones, parks, and institutional land. The subdivision-to-subdivision comparison is the fair one.

In response to community input at public hearings, the developer switched to an air-cooled closed-loop system. The approved design consumes roughly 42,500 gallons per day across the same 1,037-acre campus, or about 41 gallons per acre per day. [11b]

One question is buried in this history that deserves direct engagement. The community negotiated the project from a water-cooled design to a closed-loop air-cooled design. That is a real win. But if the original concern was based on conditions in Phoenix or figures from a poorly sited facility elsewhere, rather than a careful analysis of local hydrology and the specific design in front of the council, then the negotiation extracted a real operational concession in response to a concern that may not have been well-founded in this geography. The concession produced a better outcome. That does not mean the underlying analysis driving the negotiation was correct.

*Tier: Isolated — deliberate regulatory evasion, not representative of compliant facilities*

The QTS situation in Fayette County, Georgia, belongs in a different category. QTS used 29 million gallons of water over an estimated nine to fifteen months without the utility's knowledge. Two connections were involved: one installed without utility authorization, one not linked to QTS's billing account. Both went undetected until residents of a nearby subdivision complained about low water pressure. QTS paid the retroactive bill of $147,474 and attributed the usage to construction-phase activity. The county declined to impose additional fines. The utility director told Politico: "They're our largest customer, and we have to be partners." [13]

That outcome reflects an enforcement conflict rooted in financial dependency, not a plausible operating norm at compliant facilities. The flaw is in the enforcement framework and in the financial relationship that compromised it.

## Water Quality

Water quality and water supply are different concerns. Volume is one question. Contamination is another.

*Tier: Contested — Amazon is one defendant among 17; causation is disputed and unproven*

Amazon is one of 17 defendants in a class action over nitrate contamination affecting Morrow and Umatilla counties in Oregon. The contamination traces to decades of excess nitrogen application on farm fields, a practice involving food processors, agricultural operations, and the Port of Morrow. Amazon opened its first facility in Morrow County in 2011 and now operates 13 there. The company denies its operations contributed to groundwater contamination. In March 2026, Amazon agreed to a proposed $20.5 million settlement to fund well and water treatment improvements, without admitting liability, pending court approval. [12]

*Tier: Selective — when groundwater contamination risk is applied only to data centers, not to comparable industrial uses*

Groundwater contamination from normal operations at compliant data center facilities is not supported by published evidence. Plausible mechanisms exist (fuel oil storage, construction dewatering, cooling system discharge), but those same mechanisms exist at warehouses, manufacturing plants, and hospitals. Applying the concern selectively to data centers without comparative data is not evidence-based analysis.

## Noise

Virginia's JLARC study found that most data centers generate zero noise complaints. Problems occur at facilities sited too close to residential zones or with inadequate acoustic design. [4] This comes from the state that has studied the question more systematically than any other. The translation is direct: data center noise is a design and siting problem, not an inherent property of the building type.

*Tier: Regional — air-cooled facilities in hot climates near residential zones*

The mechanism matters. Air-cooled facilities run cooling fans continuously. In hot climates (Phoenix, Northern Virginia in summer, much of the Southeast) fans run at or near full speed for extended periods. Modern hyperscale campuses use variable-speed fans, acoustic barriers, vegetative berms, and setbacks of several hundred feet. In at least one documented case, acoustic engineering on aerodynamic impeller designs achieved a 97.5% reduction in tonal fan noise while simultaneously improving energy efficiency. [14] That technology exists, it has performance data, and it is not universally deployed. The industry is accountable for that.

*Tier: Exaggerated — siting and zoning failures attributed to the building type itself*

The Chandler, Arizona, situation illustrates what happens when a framework built for smaller industrial uses gets applied to a facility of a different scale in a different climate. A data center operating within its permitted parameters ended up in a prolonged noise dispute with adjacent residents. The facility was compliant. The zoning was inadequate. Chandler has since updated its noise ordinance. Most jurisdictions have not.

*Tier: Misattributed — when hot-climate noise profiles are applied to cold-climate liquid-cooled facilities*

In Northern Illinois, cooling fans operate at lower duty cycles for six or more months of the year because free-air economization is available. A facility running liquid or hybrid cooling in a cold climate does not produce the same noise profile as an air-cooled facility in Phoenix running at full capacity in July. Applying Chandler's noise record to a proposal in Yorkville requires ignoring that distinction.

## Thermal Effects

*Tier: Regional — documented at air-cooled facilities in Phoenix metro*

A peer-reviewed study by Arizona State University researchers measured ambient air temperature at four Phoenix-area data center facilities from June through October 2025, using vehicle-mounted sensors driven simultaneously upwind and downwind of each site. It found downwind air temperature increases of up to 4°F (2.2°C) at air-cooled facilities during summer operating conditions. The study is the first to directly measure ambient air temperature rather than relying on satellite-derived land surface temperature. [14a]

*Tier: Contested — global satellite-based preprint; causation not established, peer review not complete*

A preprint published on arXiv in March 2026 (Marinoni et al.) claimed measurable land surface temperature increases extending several miles from more than 6,000 data center sites globally. [14b] That paper has not completed peer review. It measures land surface temperature from satellite data, not ambient air temperature, and does not control for the fact that constructing a large facility converts permeable vegetation to impervious surfaces, a change that independently alters satellite-measured land surface temperature. The step-change the paper reports at facility opening coincides with when a large impervious surface appears in the satellite's field of view. The paper does not attempt to separate those two effects. [21] The Cambridge preprint's central claim may be real, substantially overstated, or largely an artifact of construction. The methodology cannot distinguish among those possibilities.

*Tier: Misattributed — when Phoenix air-cooling thermal effects are applied to cold-climate liquid-cooled facilities*

The ASU study establishes a measurable localized thermal effect at air-cooled facilities in a hot-climate, continuous-operation scenario. Beyond that specific set of conditions, the evidence for neighborhood-scale thermal impact from data center operations has not been established in peer-reviewed literature. Claiming that a data center proposal will create a neighborhood heat island in Northern Illinois, a cold climate likely running liquid or hybrid cooling, is not supported by any published evidence.

## Land and Habitat

*Tier: Selective — legitimate concern applied without comparison to realistic alternatives*

Data centers in Northern Illinois are typically sited on agricultural land. Row crop agriculture, primarily corn and soybeans, is the dominant prior use. The conversion is real, permanent, and worth evaluating honestly.

Converting the same land to a warehouse distribution campus generates more truck traffic per acre, more diesel particulate from truck engines, more noise at the property line from loading docks and refrigeration units, greater demand for emergency response services, and comparable impervious surface coverage. The data center is frequently the less intrusive industrial use when measured against what a community would realistically permit on the same parcel in the same zoning district. Yorkville's own aldermen made this argument in approving Project Cardinal, and the primary record supports it. [11b]

The Project Cardinal approval included conditions covering stormwater management, perimeter screening, and landscaping buffers. [11b] These conditions produce better measurable habitat outcomes than a standard industrial park built to minimum code.

Light Pollution: Wildlife and Habitat
Tier: Systemic — documented across multiple facility types and geographies
Artificial light at night disrupts migratory bird navigation, suppresses insect abundance, alters predator-prey encounter timing, and interferes with plant-pollinator interactions. The peer-reviewed literature on these effects is substantial. [25] Large illuminated industrial campuses contribute to artificial light at night regardless of building type. Any large 24-hour facility lit to commercial standards produces the same class of effect. The concern is legitimate for facilities sited near migratory flyways or sensitive habitat. It is addressable through Dark Sky-compliant design specifications, full-cutoff fixtures, and motion-activated lighting on non-security perimeter areas.
Tier: Selective — applied to data centers without comparison to functionally identical uses
The artificial light at night critique circulates in data center opposition without reference to the same effect produced by warehouse logistics campuses, 24-hour manufacturing facilities, highway interchanges, or big-box retail. The mechanism is identical across all of them. The opposition is not.

Light Pollution: Human Health
Tier: Systemic — the biological mechanism is established in peer-reviewed literature
Artificial light at night suppresses melatonin production and disrupts circadian rhythm. The mechanism is established physiology. In 2007 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified shift work involving circadian disruption as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A), a classification confirmed in IARC Monograph 98 (2010) and reaffirmed in Volume 124 (2020). [26] The American Medical Association adopted formal policy in 2016 specifically on high-intensity LED community lighting, citing melatonin suppression and circadian disruption as documented pathways and finding that brighter residential nighttime lighting is associated with reduced sleep times, impaired daytime functioning, and obesity. [27] Epidemiological reviews have found associations between outdoor artificial light at night and elevated rates of breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and depression across large population cohorts. [28]
Tier: Exaggerated — clinical dose levels from occupational and population-scale studies are not equivalent to residential proximity exposure
The dose question is where this concern consistently gets misrepresented in community opposition materials. The epidemiological literature establishing health effects draws on two populations: night shift workers exposed to high-intensity light for extended occupational periods, and large metropolitan populations correlated with satellite-measured aggregate light pollution across entire regions. Neither exposure scenario describes a resident living adjacent to an industrial facility at a standard setback distance.
Light intensity decreases with the square of distance. The IES and the Department of Energy have both noted that the health impacts attributed to LED lighting depend on intensity, spectral composition, duration, and timing within an individual's circadian cycle — not simply on the presence of a lit structure nearby. [27] A facility with full-cutoff fixtures at a 500-foot setback produces a materially different exposure at a bedroom window than a night shift worker under continuous overhead lighting. That distinction is absent from most advocacy framing on this topic.
Epidemiological studies of outdoor artificial light at night at the population scale also carry a methodological limitation that matters here: they measure aggregate satellite-measured light across neighborhoods or administrative areas, not the incremental contribution of a single facility to a single residence. The National Cancer Institute has noted that mixed findings from population-level studies are difficult to interpret specifically because exposure assessment at the individual level remains unresolved. [28a] No peer-reviewed study has measured health outcomes in residents specifically attributable to living adjacent to a single industrial facility.
Tier: Selective — applied to data centers; not applied to functionally identical sources
A warehouse fulfillment center operating three shifts illuminates comparable or greater exterior acreage. The circadian disruption mechanism applies identically. The opposition does not.
Tier: Contested — whether the incremental exposure from a single facility at standard setback distances produces measurable individual health outcomes
The mechanism is real. The dose-response relationship at residential proximity distances from a single industrial source has not been established in peer-reviewed literature. The gap between "the mechanism is established science" and "this facility will cause cancer in these neighbors" is the same gap that separated the PEC air quality analysis from what Virginia DEQ actually measured: a permitted ceiling presented as an operational reality.
There is no federal regulatory standard for residential light exposure from adjacent industrial facilities. That is a real regulatory gap. It is not, by itself, evidence that harm is occurring at any specific facility.

## Chemical Hazards

*Tier: Regional/Legacy — AFFF/PFAS at facilities with documented fire suppression discharge history; not yet documented as systemic across compliant current-generation facilities*

Aqueous film-forming foam is the documented concern. PFAS compounds in AFFF are associated with groundwater contamination at sites where fire suppression systems were tested or discharged. PFOA and PFOS, the long-chain PFAS historically used in AFFF, are now designated as hazardous substances under CERCLA, and EPA's concurrent drinking-water rule sets maximum contaminant levels for both at 4 parts per trillion. [15a][15b] The data center industry has been transitioning to PFAS-free suppression agents. There is no publicly tracked, independently verified timeline for completing that transition across the industry. The industry has not published one. That is an accountability failure.

*Tier: Contested — dielectric cooling fluids at current commercial scale; plausible concern, no documented harm*

Dielectric cooling fluids used in immersion cooling systems are a newer question. Immersion cooling is growing in deployment, particularly in high-density AI and HPC facilities, but lifecycle environmental data at commercial scale does not yet exist in peer-reviewed literature. The concern is plausible. It is not a documented harm at compliant facilities. Not monitoring it as deployment scales would be a mistake.

*Tier: Exaggerated — fire incidents are a real documented event class; claims that data centers are a persistent or distinctive fire hazard are not supported by industry-wide incident data*

Battery energy storage systems at data center campuses are regulated under NFPA 855 and UL 9540A. Fire is a real event class at any facility operating high-density electrical equipment. For four consecutive years, Uptime Institute's annual outage analysis found declining severity and frequency of significant outages industry-wide, with power supply remaining the leading cause of impactful outages, not fire. [16] The claim that data centers are a persistent or distinctive fire hazard is not supported by industry-wide incident data.

## Electromagnetic

*Tier: Fabricated*

The claim that data centers emit harmful electromagnetic radiation to adjacent properties has no credible evidentiary basis. Copper and fiber network infrastructure generates fields within the range of standard commercial and residential installations. This claim circulates in community opposition without citation because it does not have one.

## Where the Industry Is Wrong

The NDAs are the most corrosive practice. Senator Blumenthal's investigation in March 2026 found that 25 of 31 Virginia localities with identifiable data center relationships were operating under nondisclosure agreements that restricted their ability to share basic project information with residents. Wisconsin and Minnesota communities had similar agreements. [18b] Microsoft dropped its NDA requirements in March 2026. [17] No other major operator has publicly announced a similar policy change. Using legal agreements to prevent elected officials from disclosing deal terms to their constituents is not compatible with claiming to welcome community oversight.

"100% renewable" as currently marketed is an accounting claim, not a physical one. Annual renewable energy certificate matching means the operator purchased certificates equivalent to its annual consumption. It does not mean renewable generation is physically supplying the facility at any given moment. During peak demand periods, coal and gas generators are running on the same grid. The industry continues marketing the simpler claim.

"Water positive by 2030" is a future commitment. Presenting it as a current operational achievement is not accurate, and several major operators have done exactly that.

The Georgia job count is the clearest example of what happens when nobody checks the projections. The state published initial figures of 28,350 construction jobs and 5,471 operations jobs created by data centers in exchange for $474 million in annual tax exemptions. A January 2026 correction revised those figures to 8,505 and 1,641 respectively, less than a third of what was originally claimed, following an independent review by the Carl Vinson Institute of Government at the University of Georgia. No explanation for the discrepancy was provided. [18] Job projections from large development proposals are routinely inflated across industries. That this happens at stadiums, casinos, and Amazon distribution centers as well does not make it acceptable; it means data centers are behaving like every other large development that wants a tax deal. The Georgia auditors' error is notable because it was documented and published, not because overstated projections are unique here.

xAI's conduct in the Memphis metro area and Southaven, Mississippi, was not an oversight. It was a pattern. I found no public formal statement from major industry trade organizations condemning it.

## Where the Opposition Is Wrong

In early 2026, the Piedmont Environmental Council commissioned an air quality analysis of a proposed Vantage data center in Sterling, Virginia. The report was prepared by Michael Cork through his consultancy EmPower Analytics Group. Cork holds a doctorate in statistics. He does not hold credentials in environmental engineering or air quality analysis. The report presented findings in an environmental engineering register.

Virginia DEQ reviewed the analysis and published a formal rebuttal. DEQ found three specific methodological failures. Cork aggregated all of the facility's turbines and diesel generators into a single simultaneous emission source, even though they do not operate simultaneously except during maintenance events. He modeled maximum permitted emission levels as the operational baseline, not actual emissions, which DEQ characterized as "significantly less" than permitted limits and described as typical of data center operations. And he used outdated ambient air quality data that mischaracterized conditions near the facility. DEQ's own data showed Loudoun County ranked as one of the best localities in Virginia for population health, with no evidence that had changed since the facility opened. [19a]

PEC's response was that DEQ answered a compliance question while PEC answered a health question: what do permitted emissions mean for population health? That framing is legitimate in principle. But modeling maximum permitted emissions as if they were operational norms without clearly disclosing that assumption, and aggregating sources that never run simultaneously, are not methodological choices that can be rescued by reframing the question. The information vacuum created by missing baseline data is a real problem; filling it with a flawed analysis replaces one information gap with a misleading number. The PEC report received substantial media coverage before regulators reviewed it. The DEQ rebuttal received considerably less. [19]

Virginia DEQ has since launched a real-time ambient air monitoring network across Loudoun's data center corridor. That is the correct response to a data gap. Measurement will settle what argument has not.

The broader pattern in environmental opposition involves presenting permitted emission ceilings as operational norms, applying geographic conditions from one region to another where they do not hold, and citing atypical facilities as representative of the industry. A permit that authorizes 1,000 hours of generator operation per year does not predict that generators will run 1,000 hours per year. Virginia's JLARC found generators running at roughly 7% of permitted capacity. The ceiling and the operational reality are not the same number.

## Where Regulators Are Wrong

The generator permitting framework in Northern Virginia was not designed for 4,700 diesel units evaluated one at a time without cumulative modeling. Each permit may have been correctly issued under existing rules. The rules were not designed for this concentration, and the cumulative analysis of simultaneous activation has not been done. [4]

There are no federal standardized water metering or reporting requirements for data center facilities. The QTS case in Fayette County involved 29 million gallons drawn through connections the utility did not know existed, discovered only because residential water pressure dropped. The metering system was not designed to catch it. [13]

Pre-construction environmental baseline requirements for acoustics, air quality, and water do not exist in most jurisdictions. Without pre-construction acoustic measurements, noise disputes after a facility opens cannot be definitively resolved. Without pre-construction air quality monitoring at regional stations, it is impossible to establish whether measured changes are attributable to the facility or to background trends. The data that would make post-construction disputes resolvable is not being collected because nobody requires it.

There is no national database of data center environmental incidents or complaints. There is no framework for cumulative environmental review in any major data center geography. PFAS designation under CERCLA creates liability exposure for past contamination, but there is no systematic tracking of which facilities have completed the AFFF transition or on what timeline. Regulatory frameworks for data centers were adapted from industrial and commercial land use codes that predate the building type.

---

## Where Local Government Is Wrong

Local governments signing NDAs that restrict what elected officials can disclose to residents are not acting as regulators. They are acting as partners in limiting public information. A local official who cannot tell constituents what a data center project entails cannot represent those constituents in evaluating it. [17][18b] In March 2026, Microsoft announced it would stop requiring NDAs from local governments. This publication had called for exactly that kind of transparency the previous month. Microsoft did not cite this publication as the reason, and we are not claiming credit. The timing is what it is.

The Fayette County outcome with QTS illustrates the financial dependency problem at the local level. The county's own utility director characterized QTS as a partner because QTS is the county's largest customer. A utility that cannot enforce its own metering requirements against its largest customer has a structural conflict that no amount of enforcement authority resolves. [13]

Most jurisdictions reviewing data center proposals are using planning and zoning frameworks written for warehouses and industrial parks of a substantially different character. A hyperscale data center draws 50 to 150 permanent employees, 30 to 100 MW of electrical load, and cooling water or air requirements that may exceed what surrounding municipal infrastructure was sized to serve. These are not equivalent uses, and the frameworks applied to them do not provide equivalent protections.

Conditions that would produce resolvable disputes (pre-construction acoustic baselines, independent water capacity assessments, generator fleet NOx estimates for the local airshed) are not standard permit requirements in most jurisdictions. Some communities have figured this out. Yorkville requires continuous noise monitoring as a permit condition. Chandler rewrote its noise ordinance. Most communities do not know to ask for these things, and nobody tells them.

## What Nobody Knows

There is no controlled comparison of data center thermal, noise, or air quality impacts versus comparably sized warehouses or manufacturing facilities on matched parcels. Without that comparison, claims that data centers are uniquely harmful on any of these measures cannot be evaluated against the realistic alternative.

The ASU Phoenix study establishes a measurable ambient air temperature effect downwind of air-cooled facilities in a hot climate. Whether that effect is measurable at comparable facilities in cold climates, or at liquid-cooled facilities in any climate, has not been studied. [14a]

Per-prompt water consumption figures circulated widely in 2024 and 2025 but have not been measured consistently across a representative sample of facilities and workloads in any published peer-reviewed study. The figures that circulate are extrapolations from studies conducted under specific conditions that are not typical of the full range of deployments. [21]

Dielectric cooling fluid behavior in soil and groundwater if released at commercial scale has not been published in peer-reviewed literature. The lifecycle environmental data for immersion cooling at hyperscale does not yet exist.

Pre-construction acoustic and air quality baselines are not standard permit requirements in most jurisdictions. Both sides are asserting things that baseline measurements would answer. Neither side is pushing to require those measurements.

## The Honest Accounting

The evidence does not support blanket opposition to data centers on environmental grounds. It also does not support the industry's framing that the concerns are largely invented.

What the evidence shows is that environmental impact from this building type is highly conditional. It depends on the grid, the cooling technology, the climate, the proximity to residences, the density of the local generator fleet, and whether the regulatory framework in place was designed with a facility of this scale in mind. Most of it was not. The generator permitting that produces Northern Virginia's cumulative NOx exposure was designed for one turbine at a time. The water metering that missed QTS's 29 million gallons was built for residential accounts. The zoning that put a Chandler facility within earshot of homes was written before facilities of this scale existed. These failures are not anomalies. They are the predictable outcome of applying frameworks built for a different era to a building type that did not exist when the rules were written.

The same condition applies to the opposition. The claims that circulate in Illinois community meetings are not Illinois claims. They are Virginia's generator problem, Phoenix's water problem, Oregon's nitrate contamination lawsuit, and Tennessee's air permit problem, stripped of the geographic conditions that made them true. That is not advocacy. It is a description of how evidence travels faster than the context that gives it meaning.

Both sides are operating with incomplete data in a regulatory environment that was never designed to produce the measurements needed to settle the argument. The industry withholds information through NDAs and opaque sustainability marketing. Opponents cite permit ceilings as operating norms and apply conditions from Phoenix to proposals in DeKalb. Regulators issue individual permits without cumulative modeling. Local governments approve projects without the pre-construction baselines that would make post-construction disputes resolvable. The result is a debate where the loudest voices fill the vacuum left by missing data, and the communities bearing the actual costs are left without the tools to evaluate what is happening to them.

The technical arguments will keep shifting. The industry is actively pursuing nuclear power purchase agreements as a path to closing the carbon objection. Google signed the first corporate SMR purchase agreement with Kairos Power in October 2024. [24a] Microsoft signed a 20-year PPA with Constellation Energy in September 2024 to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1, with power expected by 2027. [24b] Amazon committed to back 5 GW of X-energy SMR projects in October 2024. [24c] Meta issued a request for proposals for 1 to 4 GW of new nuclear capacity. [24d] If those deployments materialize on their projected timelines, the grid emissions argument against data centers weakens substantially for the facilities drawing on that supply. It will not end the debate. The same communities currently raising carbon concerns will find new objections: waste storage, siting proximity, construction timelines. Some of those objections will have merit. The pattern is worth recognizing: each time a technical solution closes one argument, the argument migrates to the next available concern. The solution is not to wait for the perfect technical answer. It is to build the baseline data, the cumulative modeling, and the permit conditions that make the actual impacts measurable, so that when the next argument arrives, the answer does not have to be guesswork.
