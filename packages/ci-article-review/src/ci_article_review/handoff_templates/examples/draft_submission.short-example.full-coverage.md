# DeKalb County's Broadband Money Is Approved. Its Permitting Office Isn't Ready For It.

*Mike Hammett has operated ISP infrastructure in Northern Illinois for two decades and co-founded FD-IX. He holds no financial interest in any BEAD subgrantee or broadband grant recipient.*

## TL;DR

- Illinois's federal broadband grant money is real and already approved — NTIA cleared the state's Initial Proposal in June 2024, unlocking access to more than $1 billion of the program's $42.45 billion national total. [1][2]
- The bottleneck nobody's budgeting for is pole attachments. In July 2026, broadband providers told the FCC directly that Illinois is one of six states whose pole-attachment rules and enforcement lag behind states like Maine and Pennsylvania. [3]
- If the state's regulatory regime is already drawing that criticism, DeKalb County's permitting office — which has never processed a project at BEAD's volume — isn't going to move any faster than the system it operates inside of.
- This piece is about deployment mechanics, not about whether $42.45 billion is enough money nationally, or whether affordability matters as much as availability. Those are real questions. They're a different article.

## The Money Part Is Actually Simple

Start with what isn't in dispute. BEAD — the Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment program — is a $42.45 billion federal grant program funded under the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and run by NTIA, built to close the country's remaining broadband gaps county by county. [2] Illinois cleared its first real hurdle in June 2024, when NTIA approved the state's Initial Proposal and unlocked access to more than $1 billion for planning and eventual construction. [1]

That is the part every press release covers, and it's the part county boards tend to treat as the finish line. It isn't. Initial Proposal approval means a state can start selecting subgrantees and writing checks against its allocation — it does not mean a single strand of fiber gets built. The construction clock that comes with BEAD funding starts running well before most counties have a permitting process built for the volume of work headed their way.

## Overview

Getting a broadband build from "funded" to "in the ground" runs through a sequence familiar to anyone who has worked utility infrastructure: engineering design, pole-attachment applications, make-ready work by the pole owner, right-of-way permits from the county or township, and finally construction. Each step has its own timeline, its own approving authority, and its own way of stalling a project that already has money behind it.

## Where the Clock Actually Runs Into a Wall

In today's broadband policy conversation, funding announcements get the headlines while permitting timelines get a shrug — which is somewhat backwards, since permitting is where a fully funded project can still sit for a year doing nothing. The problem, in short, is that pole-attachment negotiations routinely take longer than a construction schedule assumes. Survey backlogs are frequently cited as a leading cause of delay, and overlash construction techniques are widely understood to cut permitting timelines by roughly half. The solution, in most proposals under discussion, involves streamlined self-help remedies and firmer FCC-enforced deadlines. It remains to be seen whether any of that reaches Illinois before the clock most counties are already on runs out.

Providers aren't being quiet about which states have the problem. In comments filed with the FCC in July 2026, six states — Delaware, New Jersey, Illinois, Massachusetts, Michigan, and California — were scrutinized for outdated pole-attachment rules and weak enforcement, against Maine, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Ohio held up as the models other states should copy. GoNetspeed, an ISP that has passed roughly 770,000 locations, went further and named Illinois and Michigan specifically as lacking critical elements of an effective pole-attachment regulatory regime. [3] The company's chief legal counsel, James Hoare, argued to the FCC that a state's certification to regulate its own pole attachments should be treated as invalid once it stops maintaining the rules federal law requires it to enforce. [3]

If the state's own regulatory framework is drawing that kind of criticism, DeKalb County's permitting office — which has never processed a project at BEAD's volume, and which reviews pole and right-of-way applications with the same staff that handles every other county infrastructure request — is not going to outperform the system it operates inside of. A county permitting office is not a separate machine from the state framework it operates under; it is downstream of it, and downstream offices don't typically move faster than the pipe feeding them.

A few things are worth keeping in view here:

- The construction clock started at Initial Proposal approval, not at county readiness.
- Pole-attachment negotiations run on the utility's schedule as much as the applicant's.
- Illinois has been named specifically, not generically, in the July 2026 filings.
- DeKalb County has not published its own permit-processing timeline.
- None of the above is a reason to expect a faster outcome than the state average.

## What Nobody's Confirmed Yet

As of this writing, Illinois's BEAD Final Proposal — the step that actually authorizes subgrantee selection and releases construction dollars, as distinct from the 2024 Initial Proposal approval — does not appear to have cleared NTIA, based on industry tracking of the program's 56 state and territory entities. That status changes monthly and is worth a fresh check against NTIA's own BEAD dashboard before anyone treats it as settled either way.

The subscription side of the gap is easier to point to than it is to source precisely at the county level. According to the Census Bureau's ACCESS BROADBAND dashboard, 87.4 percent of DeKalb County households had a broadband subscription as of the dashboard's most recent estimate — a number worth keeping next to the pole-attachment story, since a county can finish a BEAD-funded build and still show a stubborn adoption gap if the subscription side isn't addressed separately. [4]

The comparison problem compounds over time. The Census Bureau's own documentation on how its American Community Survey table redesigns changed broadband-subscription estimates across geography vintages is the reference point for whether this year's county figure is even comparable to last year's. [5]

## What This Piece Isn't

This is a piece about deployment mechanics — money, poles, permits, and the sequence between funding approval and a household getting service. It is not a piece about whether $42.45 billion is the right amount of money to close the national digital divide, and it is not a piece about affordability: whether a household that gets a fiber line run past it can actually afford to subscribe is a separate and, by most measures, at least as important a question as whether the line gets built at all. That question deserves its own reporting, with its own sources on subscription pricing and low-income program enrollment. Folding it in here would shortchange both arguments.

## Conclusion

Illinois's broadband money is real, it's approved, and it's substantial. What determines whether it turns into service for DeKalb County's unserved households isn't the size of the federal check — it's whether pole attachment and permitting keep pace with a construction clock that started running the moment the state's proposal was approved. Only time will tell whether the county's permitting office can keep up.
