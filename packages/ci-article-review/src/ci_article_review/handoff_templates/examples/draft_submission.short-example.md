# Congress Fixed the Wrong Half of the Clock Problem

## TL;DR

- The Sunshine Protection Act, passed by the U.S. Senate by unanimous consent
  on March 15, 2022, would have made daylight saving time permanent. It never
  got a House vote. [1]
- The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's own 2020 position statement argues
  for the opposite fix: permanent standard time, not permanent daylight time,
  on circadian-health grounds. [2]
- Evidence that evening daylight reduces street crime is real and worth
  taking seriously. It is not, by itself, a health argument, and it is not
  the argument most permanent-DST advocates think they're making. [4]
- No state can adopt permanent daylight time on its own. Federal law allows
  states to opt out to permanent standard time, which is why Arizona and
  Hawaii already have, but opting in to permanent daylight time requires an
  act of Congress. [5]

---

## The Core Mismatch

Ask someone if they'd like to stop changing the clocks twice a year and the
answer is almost always yes. Ask them which direction to freeze the clock in,
and the conversation gets a lot shorter, because most people have never heard
that sleep researchers and daylight-saving advocates are arguing for opposite
outcomes.

The Sunshine Protection Act picked a side: lighter evenings, darker mornings,
permanent, nationwide. It passed the Senate by unanimous consent, which
sounds like consensus, then sat in the House without a vote for the rest of
that Congress. [1] It's been reintroduced since. The politics of the bill get
covered every time this comes up. The medical objection to its substance
rarely does.

## What the Sleep Evidence Actually Says

It's worth being direct about this: the circadian case is not a close call.
The body's internal clock is set primarily by morning light. Push sunrise
later, which is what permanent daylight time does everywhere except the
peak of summer, and you push the whole population's internal clock later
along with it — every single morning, all year, not just for a week after
the spring transition. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2020 position
statement lays this out plainly and recommends the country settle on
permanent standard time instead. [2]

The clearest illustration of the transition's cost, not the permanent
schedule's cost, comes from a Michigan hospital system's admissions data.
Sandhu, Seth, and Gurm found a jump in heart attack admissions the Monday
after the spring-forward transition and a drop the Tuesday after the
fall-back transition. [3] The Michigan data doesn't just show a correlation
between the spring transition and a bad Monday — it demonstrates that the
transition itself causes a measurable spike in heart attacks nationwide, and
that causal mechanism is the strongest single argument for eliminating the
transition altogether, in either direction.

That last sentence overclaims what one hospital system's admissions data can
support, and it should. A single-system association across a few transition
years is evidence that the spring shift correlates with a short-term rise in
admissions there. It is not evidence of a nationwide causal mechanism, and
treating it as one is exactly the kind of leap the rest of this piece is
arguing against when other people make it about crime statistics or traffic
counts.

## The Case the Other Side Isn't Wrong About

Doleac and Sanders looked at what happened to robbery rates when the U.S.
extended daylight saving time by about four weeks starting in 2007, and found
evening robbery fell measurably once the extra hour of light moved into
robbery-prone commute hours. [4] That's a real natural experiment with a
real effect, and dismissing it because it's inconvenient for the
sleep-medicine argument would be the same move in reverse.

What it isn't is a health argument. Retailers and tourism boards have been
the most consistent institutional supporters of permanent daylight time for
exactly this reason — longer light in the hours people shop and travel. It's
a different kind of evidence than a circadian-health claim, and the debate
mostly treats them as interchangeable justifications for the same
conclusion, when the crime and economic evidence supports one direction and
the sleep evidence supports the other.

## Why No State Can Just Pick One

The Uniform Time Act of 1966 gives states exactly one way to opt out of
daylight saving time: adopt permanent standard time, the way Arizona
(outside the Navajo Nation) and Hawaii already have. [5] It gives states no
matching way to opt in to permanent daylight time — that requires Congress
to change the underlying law, which is what the Sunshine Protection Act
would have done. At least nineteen states have passed contingent
permanent-daylight-time legislation, per NCSL's tracker, and every one of
those laws is a formal request sitting in front of the same House vote that
never happened in 2022. [6]

## What This Piece Doesn't Settle

This is a health-evidence argument, not a full cost-benefit accounting. It
doesn't put a dollar figure on the retail and tourism case for evening light,
and a reader who weighs regional economic activity above sleep-medicine
guidance could reasonably land on permanent daylight time anyway. What this
piece does argue is narrower and, I think, harder to dispute: whichever side
wins, it should win on its own evidence, not on the vague sense that ending
the twice-yearly change is self-evidently good regardless of which direction
it lands.
